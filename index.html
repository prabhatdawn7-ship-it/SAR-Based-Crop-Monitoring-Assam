Objectives

Why radar, and why Assam

Assam's main rice crop grows under monsoon cloud for months. Optical satellites often see nothing usable in that window. Synthetic Aperture Radar sees through cloud, day or night, which makes it the right sensor for tracking the kharif season here.

Build a cloud-free monthly view

Create median Sentinel-1 composites for each month from June to October 2025, plus a full-season composite, for all of Assam.

Explore by district

Let anyone select a district, zoom to it and compare how its backscatter changes as the season moves from land preparation to maturity.

Track the temporal signal

Plot mean backscatter for every acquisition date so the crop calendar shows up as a curve: the flooding dip, the growth rise and the plateau.

Lay the ground for classification

Use VV and VH behaviour to understand which signals separate rice, tea, forest and water, as a first step toward a trained crop-type map.

Study area

The whole state of Assam, district by district

The study area is the entire state of Assam, from Dhubri on the Bangladesh border to Tinsukia in the far east, and south through the Barak Valley to Cachar. Every district polygon in the boundary asset is analysed, and each one can be selected on its own in the app.

Assam is a monsoon-fed landscape of floodplains, wetlands, forest and plantation. Rice dominates the cropped area, and the main sali (winter) rice is transplanted during the monsoon and harvested at the end of the year. Tea estates, riverine sandbars, wetlands (beels) and forest share the same valleys, and each scatters radar differently.

Data and method

From raw radar scenes to a district view

The full workflow runs in Google Earth Engine. Each step below matches a block of the app's code.

  1. BoundariesLoad the Assam state outline and district polygons as Earth Engine assets.
  2. FilterSentinel-1 GRD, IW mode, scenes carrying both VV and VH, inside Assam, Jun 1 to Nov 1, 2025.
  3. CompositeMedian of all scenes in the chosen month or full season, for the chosen polarization.
  4. Clip and displayClip to the selected district and stretch with a fixed dB range on a four-colour ramp.
  5. Time seriesMean backscatter per acquisition date, reduced at 1 km, drawn as a line chart.

Interpretation

What the colours are telling you

The map shows radar backscatter, not crop type. These three ideas turn the colours into land-surface meaning.

Mirror effect · dark blue

Water reflects the signal away

Calm water and freshly flooded paddies act like a mirror and send energy away from the satellite. In July they read as the darkest pixels. A district that darkens sharply from June to July is a strong sign of transplanted rice.

Volume scattering · VH rises

Growing plants brighten VH

As rice tillers and canopy build up, the signal bounces around inside the leaves and stems, and cross-polarized VH climbs by several decibels from transplanting toward heading. That rise is the crop's growth curve.

Stable and bright · sand to red

Trees, tea and towns stay high

Forest and tea canopies scatter strongly all season, so their curves are flat. Buildings reflect double-bounce energy and show as the brightest red. Flat, high curves mean the surface is not a seasonal crop.

Using the app

Four controls, one question

  1. Study district

    Pick a district, or keep All Assam. The map zooms to it and outlines it.

  2. Observation period

    Choose a month or the full season to change the median composite.

  3. Polarization

    VH for vegetation growth, VV for surface and moisture contrast.

  4. Temporal chart

    Read the date-by-date mean. Look for the July dip and the September rise.

Limitations

Read with care

  • Explorer, not a classifier. Colours are backscatter levels. No crop labels or accuracy assessment are produced yet.
  • No speckle filtering. Median compositing reduces speckle, but single-month views can still look grainy.
  • Mixed orbits. Ascending and descending passes are combined, so viewing geometry varies between dates.
  • District means at 1 km. The chart averages every land cover in the district, which dampens the rice signal where rice is a small share.
